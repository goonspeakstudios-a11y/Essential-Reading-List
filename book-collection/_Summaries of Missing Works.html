Summaries of Missing Works

Short summaries, written from general knowledge, of the reading-list works that couldn't be found as a free download. They're for orientation, not a substitute for the books. Sections follow the reading list's numbering.

1. The Trivium

Priscian — Institutes of Grammar

Latin, c. 500 AD, 18 books

The largest Latin grammar to survive from antiquity, written in Constantinople by a teacher of Latin to Greek speakers. Books 1–16 treat sounds, letters, syllables and the eight parts of speech (noun, verb, participle, pronoun, preposition, adverb, interjection, conjunction), with heavy illustration from Virgil, Terence, Cicero and others. Books 17–18 treat syntax, closely following the Greek grammarian Apollonius Dyscolus. It was the advanced grammar textbook of the Middle Ages ("Priscianus maior") and a key source for medieval linguistic theory, including the speculative grammarians.

Servius — Commentary on Virgil

Latin, late 4th century

A line-by-line school commentary on the Aeneid, Eclogues and Georgics. Servius explains grammar, meter and rare words, but also supplies mythology, Roman religious law, antiquarian lore and alternative readings. It preserves fragments of lost authors and much of what is known about early Roman ritual. An expanded version ("Servius Danielis") adds material from the older commentator Donatus. It shaped how Virgil was read, as both poet and encyclopedia, for a thousand years.

Alcuin of York — Disputatio with Pippin

Latin, c. 790

A teaching dialogue between Alcuin and Charlemagne's young son Pippin, built from rapid riddling questions and answers: "What is the sun? The splendor of the world... What is winter? The exile of summer." Each answer is a small metaphor or kenning, training the pupil in definition, wordplay and memory. It ends with true riddles. It shows the Carolingian classroom at its most playful and reflects the Anglo-Saxon love of riddles.

Donatus — Ars Minor; Ars Maior

Latin, mid-4th century

Aelius Donatus, Jerome's teacher in Rome, wrote two grammars. The Ars Minor walks through the eight parts of speech in question-and-answer form ("How many parts of speech are there? Eight...") and was so universal that "a donat" came to mean any elementary primer; it was among the first books ever printed. The Ars Maior covers sounds, syllables, meter and accent, the parts of speech in more depth, and a final book on faults and ornaments of style (barbarism, solecism, figures and tropes), which made it a bridge from grammar into rhetoric.

Boethius — On Topical Differences

Latin, c. 520

A treatise on "topics", the stock places from which arguments are drawn (from definition, from cause, from the greater and the lesser, from contraries, and so on). Boethius compares the systems of Themistius and Cicero, shows how they map onto each other, and distinguishes dialectical from rhetorical topics. It became the standard medieval textbook on how to find arguments and underlies the medieval theory of consequences and the later logic of the schools.

Hermogenes of Tarsus — On Types of Style

Greek, 2nd century

Hermogenes analyzes prose style into seven master qualities: clarity, grandeur, beauty, rapidity, character, sincerity and force, most of them with subtypes (grandeur, for instance, includes solemnity, asperity, vehemence, brilliance, florescence and abundance). For each he specifies the thoughts, approaches, diction, figures, clause lengths, word order and rhythms that produce it. Demosthenes is the model who blends them all. It was the core of Byzantine rhetorical education and influenced Renaissance stylistics.

Bede — On the Art of Metre; On Figures and Tropes

Latin, c. 700

Two short textbooks for monastic students. The first explains Latin quantitative verse: syllable length, feet, the hexameter and other meters, drawing examples from Christian poets such as Sedulius and Arator rather than pagans. The second catalogs schemes and tropes (metaphor, allegory, irony and so on) but illustrates every one from Scripture, arguing that the Bible surpasses pagan literature in eloquence as well as truth.

2. Mathematics, Nature & the Physical Order

Benoit Mandelbrot — The Fractal Geometry of Nature

English, 1982

Mandelbrot argues that clouds, coastlines, mountains and trees are not smooth Euclidean shapes but rough, self-similar forms whose detail repeats at every scale. He introduces the fractal, a shape with fractional dimension, and applies it to coastline length (which grows as the ruler shrinks), river networks, galaxy clustering, turbulence and noise. Richly illustrated, it popularized the Mandelbrot set and made "roughness" a subject of mathematics.

Benoit Mandelbrot — The (Mis)Behavior of Markets

English, 2004, with Richard L. Hudson

A critique of modern finance theory, which assumes price changes are independent and bell-curve distributed. Mandelbrot shows that real markets have fat tails (huge moves far more often than the bell curve allows), long memory, volatility clustering and fractal scaling across time frames. So standard risk models badly understate danger. He proposes multifractal models instead. The book anticipated the 2008 crisis critique.

Werner Heisenberg — Physics and Philosophy

English, 1958 (Gifford Lectures)

Heisenberg explains the Copenhagen interpretation of quantum theory for general readers: the uncertainty relations, the role of observation, complementarity, and the idea that the wave function describes potentiality ("potentia", in an Aristotelian sense) rather than objective things. He traces physics' relation to Greek atomism, Descartes and Kant, discusses how the language of classical physics must still be used to describe quantum experiments, and reflects on science's place in modern culture.

Erwin Schrödinger — What Is Life?

English, 1944

A physicist asks how living things maintain order against the tendency toward disorder. Schrödinger argues that organisms feed on "negative entropy" from their surroundings, and that heredity must be stored in an "aperiodic crystal", a molecule whose irregular structure encodes information stably enough to survive thermal noise. The idea influenced Watson and Crick. An epilogue muses on free will and the unity of consciousness.

Richard Feynman — The Character of Physical Law

English, 1965 (Messenger Lectures)

Seven informal lectures on what physical laws are like. Using gravitation as the model case, Feynman discusses the role of mathematics, the great conservation principles, symmetry, the distinction between past and future, probability and uncertainty in quantum mechanics, and how new laws are found (guess, compute the consequences, compare with experiment). It stresses that nature is simple in its deep laws yet strange.

Richard Feynman — QED: The Strange Theory of Light and Matter

English, 1985

Feynman explains quantum electrodynamics without equations, using little rotating arrows ("probability amplitudes") that are added and squared to give probabilities. With them he accounts for partial reflection from glass, mirrors, lenses and diffraction, then electron-photon interactions drawn as Feynman diagrams. He admits the theory is absurd by common sense yet agrees with experiment to extraordinary precision.

James Gleick — Chaos: Making a New Science

English, 1987

A narrative history of chaos theory told through its scientists: Edward Lorenz's weather model and the "butterfly effect" (sensitive dependence on initial conditions), strange attractors, Feigenbaum's universal constants in period-doubling, Mandelbrot's fractals, and chaos in dripping faucets, heartbeats and populations. The theme is that simple deterministic systems can behave unpredictably, and that disorder has hidden order.

G. H. Hardy — A Mathematician's Apology

English, 1940

An aging Cambridge mathematician defends his life's work. Hardy argues that pure mathematics is justified by beauty, like painting or poetry, not usefulness. Real mathematics is "harmless and innocent," and its best results show seriousness, generality, depth, unexpectedness, inevitability and economy (examples: Euclid on the infinity of primes, the irrationality of the square root of two). It is also a melancholy reflection on creative decline and mathematics as a young person's game.

Primo Levi — The Periodic Table

Italian, 1975

Twenty-one autobiographical stories, each titled after a chemical element that anchors it: argon for his Piedmontese Jewish ancestors, zinc and iron for student years and friendship, gold for his partisan capture, cerium for survival in Auschwitz, chromium and vanadium for postwar work as an industrial chemist. Chemistry becomes a moral discipline of patience and honesty with matter. It ends with a carbon atom's journey through the world and into the writer's hand.

3. The Quadrivium

Alcuin of York — Problems to Sharpen the Young

Latin, c. 800

About fifty-three word puzzles with solutions, the oldest mathematical puzzle collection in Latin. They include the wolf, goat and cabbage river crossing, jealous husbands crossing a river, a ladder with steps in arithmetic progression, dividing casks, and a problem requiring the sum 1+2+...+100. Some are trick questions. It reflects Carolingian classroom arithmetic and the love of teaching through play.

Ptolemy — Harmonics

Greek, 2nd century

A treatise that tries to reconcile reason and hearing in music. Ptolemy criticizes the Pythagoreans for ignoring perception and the Aristoxenians for ignoring ratios, and uses the monochord to test tuning systems, proposing his own divisions of the tetrachord (including the "syntonic diatonic", close to just intonation). Book 3 maps musical structures onto the soul and the heavens, a key source for the "music of the spheres."

Macrobius — Commentary on the Dream of Scipio

Latin, early 5th century

Taking Cicero's short dream narrative as a text, Macrobius builds a Neoplatonic encyclopedia: a classification of dreams (enigmatic, prophetic, oracular, nightmare, apparition), number mysticism, the soul's descent through the planetary spheres and its return, the structure of the cosmos, the music of the spheres, the earth's zones and oceans, and the soul's immortality. It was one of the main channels through which Platonism and cosmology reached the Latin Middle Ages.

16. The Church Fathers

Gregory of Nyssa — The Life of Moses

Greek, c. 390

Part one retells Moses' life from Exodus and Numbers. Part two reads it spiritually as the soul's ascent to God: the burning bush as the light of truth, Egypt as passions, crossing the sea as baptism, the manna and the rock as Christ, the ascent of Sinai into darkness as the discovery that God is beyond knowing. Its central idea is epektasis: perfection is endless progress, since the infinite God can never be exhausted.

Cassiodorus — Institutions of Divine and Secular Learning

Latin, c. 560

A reading guide and curriculum written for the monks of Vivarium, the monastery Cassiodorus founded after retiring from Ostrogothic government. Book 1 lists the books of Scripture, the best commentators and the practices of monastic study, including the careful copying of manuscripts. Book 2 summarizes the seven liberal arts (grammar, rhetoric, dialectic, arithmetic, music, geometry, astronomy) as tools for understanding Scripture. It helped make monasteries the keepers of classical learning.

Maximus the Confessor — The Ambigua

Greek, 7th century

Explanations of difficult passages in Gregory of Nazianzus and Pseudo-Dionysius that grow into a full theology of creation and deification. Key ideas: the divine Logos contains the logoi (inner principles) of all created things; Christ's union of divine and human natures reconciles the divisions of reality (God and creation, mind and senses, heaven and earth, paradise and world, male and female); and humanity's vocation is to become god by grace.

Maximus the Confessor — Four Centuries on Love

Greek, 7th century

Four hundred short numbered sayings on the spiritual life. Love of God and neighbor is the goal; it is reached by freeing the mind from passionate attachment to things and thoughts. Maximus analyzes how passions arise from misuse of things, how self-love is the root of vice, and how dispassion, prayer and knowledge lead to love. It is a standard text of Eastern spirituality in the Philokalia.

17. Medieval Theology, Philosophy & Mysticism

Peter Abelard — Sic et Non

Latin, c. 1120

A collection of 158 theological questions (is faith based on reason? is God the cause of evil? may one lie?), each followed by quotations from Scripture and the Fathers that seem to say yes and others that seem to say no. Abelard does not resolve them. His prologue gives rules for reconciling authorities: check the text's authenticity, consider changed word meanings and contexts, distinguish senses. The method of setting authorities against each other became the backbone of scholastic disputation.

Hugh of St. Victor — Didascalicon

Latin, c. 1125

A guide to what to read, in what order, and how. Hugh divides knowledge into theoretical, practical, mechanical (weaving, arms, navigation, agriculture, hunting, medicine, theater) and logical arts, notably giving the mechanical arts a place. He describes the reader's virtues: humility, zeal for learning, quiet, and memory trained by ordering. The last books teach how to read Scripture on historical, allegorical and moral levels. Its motto: learn everything; nothing is superfluous.

Albert the Great — On the Soul; Commentaries on Aristotle

Latin, 13th century

Albert set out to make all of Aristotle intelligible to Latin readers by paraphrasing and expanding every work, adding his own observations in natural science (botany, zoology, minerals). His De anima discusses the soul as form of the body, the senses, imagination and the intellect, engaging Avicenna and Averroes on the agent and possible intellect, and defends individual immortality. His encyclopedic commentaries prepared the ground for his student Thomas Aquinas.

John Duns Scotus — Ordinatio

Latin, c. 1300

Scotus' revised commentary on Peter Lombard's Sentences, his main work. Signature doctrines: being is univocal (said in the same sense of God and creatures, making natural theology possible); individuals are individuated by a positive "thisness" (haecceitas); the formal distinction; the primacy of will and love over intellect; a rigorous proof of God as the infinite first being; and the Immaculate Conception. He is famously subtle ("the Subtle Doctor").

John Climacus — The Ladder of Divine Ascent

Greek, c. 600

Written by the abbot of Sinai, it describes monastic life as a ladder of thirty rungs (matching Christ's hidden years). It begins with renunciation, detachment and exile, moves through obedience, repentance and remembrance of death, the struggle against specific passions (anger, lust, avarice, vainglory, pride), then virtues such as simplicity, humility and discernment, and ends with stillness (hesychia), prayer, dispassion and love. It is read in Orthodox monasteries every Lent.

Symeon the New Theologian — Discourses; Hymns of Divine Love

Greek, c. 1000

Symeon, a Byzantine abbot, insisted that Christians can and should consciously experience God, seen as uncreated light, in this life. His discourses to his monks call for tears, repentance, obedience to a spiritual father and ardent prayer. His hymns are autobiographical poems describing visions of divine light and union with Christ, often in bold, sensual imagery. Controversial in his lifetime, he became a pillar of Orthodox mystical theology.

Gregory Palamas — The Triads

Greek, 1330s–40s

A defense of the hesychast monks of Mount Athos, who practiced the Jesus Prayer with bodily techniques and claimed to see divine light. Against Barlaam of Calabria, Palamas distinguishes God's unknowable essence from his uncreated energies, in which God truly shares himself. The light seen by saints (like the light of Christ's Transfiguration) is these energies, so real union with God is possible without confusing creature and Creator. The body, too, takes part in prayer.

Jean Gerson — On Mystical Theology

Latin, 1402–07

The chancellor of the University of Paris treats mysticism as a discipline that can be studied. Speculative mystical theology analyzes the soul's powers and defines mystical theology as experiential knowledge of God through the embrace of unitive love. Practical mystical theology gives advice on preparing for contemplation: suitable temperament, place, time, posture and avoiding excess. He warns against false visions and pantheistic excess.

Joachim of Fiore — Exposition on the Apocalypse

Latin, c. 1200

A Calabrian abbot's reading of Revelation as a key to all history. Joachim divides history into three ages: of the Father (the married, the Old Testament law), of the Son (the clergy, the Gospel), and a coming age of the Holy Spirit (monks, spiritual understanding, contemplation and freedom). He uses number patterns and elaborate figures to predict the transition. His ideas later inspired radical Franciscans and many later theories of historical progress.

14. The Warrior's Way

Yamamoto Tsunetomo — Hagakure

Japanese, dictated c. 1709–1716

Sayings of a retired retainer of the Nabeshima clan, recorded by a young samurai, Tashiro Tsuramoto, over seven years. The famous core idea is that the way of the samurai is found in death: by resolving each morning to be already dead, a retainer acts without hesitation or self-interest. It stresses total devotion to one's lord and clan, instant decisive action over calculation, constant readiness, and attention to small details of appearance and conduct. It mixes stern ideals with gossip, anecdotes, practical etiquette and nostalgia for a fiercer past. Largely unknown until the 20th century, it was later heavily promoted by nationalists.

Miyamoto Musashi — The Book of Five Rings

Japanese, 1645

Written by an undefeated duelist near the end of his life, in five scrolls named after the elements. Earth sets out his strategy as a craft, like carpentry. Water covers the practical technique of the sword: posture, gaze, grip, footwork, and a relaxed but ready mind. Fire covers tactics in combat, such as seizing the initiative, using terrain and light, and upsetting the opponent's rhythm. Wind criticizes other schools for fixation on long swords, flashy moves or rigid stances. The Void describes the state beyond technique, clear and free of doubt. Throughout, the same principles apply to one-on-one duels and to armies. Musashi urges constant training and study of every art.

Tao Yuanming — Collected Poems

Chinese, c. 365–427

Tao Yuanming resigned from official life to farm in his home district. His poems praise simple rural life: tending fields and chrysanthemums, drinking wine with neighbors, reading, watching birds return at dusk, and accepting poverty and death with calm. Famous works include "Returning to Live in the Country," the "Drinking Wine" series, and the prose "Peach Blossom Spring," about a hidden utopian valley. His plain diction and honest temperament made him the model of the recluse-poet for later Chinese literature.

18. The Rabbinic & Kabbalistic Tradition

Abraham Abulafia — The Light of the Intellect (Or ha-Sekhel)

Hebrew, c. 1285

Abulafia founded "prophetic" or ecstatic Kabbalah. The book combines Maimonides' philosophy of the intellect with techniques for combining and permuting Hebrew letters and divine names, joined to breathing, chanting and head movements. The goal is to loosen the mind from ordinary images, so that the soul unites with the Active Intellect and receives prophecy. It is one of the most systematic Jewish manuals of meditation.

Sefer Raziel HaMalakh

Hebrew and Aramaic, compiled 13th century, printed 1701

A composite magical and cosmological book, attributed by legend to the angel Raziel, who gave it to Adam. It contains angelology, the names of angels governing times and heavens, astrology, speculations on the Divine Name and creation (much of it drawn from older mystical texts), and amulets and incantations for protection. Printed copies were long kept in homes as a charm against fire and against harm to women in childbirth.

Isaac Luria and Chaim Vital — Etz Chaim (The Tree of Life)

Hebrew, 16th century, printed 1782

Chaim Vital's systematic record of Isaac Luria's teachings in Safed, the foundation of Lurianic Kabbalah. Core doctrines: tzimtzum (God's self-contraction to make space for creation); the ray of light entering that space and forming the sefirot; the shattering of the vessels (shevirat ha-kelim), which scattered sparks of holiness among the husks of evil; the partzufim (divine "faces" or configurations); and tikkun, the repair of the world that human deeds and prayer bring about, culminating in redemption.

Martin Buber — I and Thou

German original, 1923 (downloaded separately in German)

Buber distinguishes two basic attitudes. In I–It relations we experience and use things and people as objects. In I–Thou relations we meet another whole being in mutual presence, without purpose or analysis. Modern life is dominated by I–It. Every true relationship, with a person, nature or a work of art, glimpses the eternal Thou, God, who can only be addressed, never described. Love, community and faith depend on dialogue.

Abraham Joshua Heschel — The Sabbath

English, 1951

Heschel argues that Judaism is a religion of time rather than space. Technical civilization conquers space and things. The Sabbath, a "palace in time," is a day for being rather than having or making, when a person stops dominating the world and receives it as a gift, glimpsing eternity. Drawing on rabbinic legend and mysticism, he portrays the Sabbath as a bride and queen and as a foretaste of the world to come.

Abraham Joshua Heschel — God in Search of Man

English, 1955

A philosophy of Judaism built on three ways to God: through wonder at the world (radical amazement, the sense of the ineffable), through the Bible (revelation as God's concern for humanity, not a human discovery), and through sacred deeds (the commandments, where one learns by doing). Religion begins not with humanity's quest for God but with God's pursuit of humanity. Heschel defends prophetic faith against both dry rationalism and empty ritualism.

19. The Arabic & Persian World

Rumi — Fihi Ma Fihi (It Is What It Is)

Persian, 13th century

Prose records of Rumi's talks with disciples and visitors, compiled after his death. Informal and conversational, they cover prayer, love, the teacher's role, the difference between outward form and inner meaning, and the soul's longing for God, using parables and humor. It is the prose companion to the Masnavi and reveals Rumi as a teacher.

Rudaki — Poems

Persian, 10th century

Rudaki, court poet of the Samanids in Bukhara, is called the father of Persian poetry. Legend credits him with a huge output, but only about a thousand couplets survive: lyric and wine poems, praise of rulers, reflections on aging and the passing of fortune, and a famous poem on the lost pleasures of youth. His clear, musical style set the standard for classical Persian verse.

Nasir Khusraw — Diwan

Persian, 11th century

After a spiritual crisis Nasir Khusraw became an Ismaili missionary, and his poetry is mostly moral and philosophical. He rejects the frivolous courtly lyric and instead urges reason, knowledge and ethical living, denounces the corruption of rulers and scholars, and expounds Ismaili esoteric ideas about the hidden meaning of revelation. The poems are written in a strong, dense style, often from exile in the Pamir mountains.

Al-Jahiz — The Book of Animals

Arabic, 9th century

A vast, rambling work that uses animals (dogs and roosters, insects, birds, snakes, camels) as a starting point for everything: zoology from observation and hearsay, poetry and proverbs, theology of design in creation, anthropology and humor. Al-Jahiz discusses adaptation to environment, food chains and animal communication. It is as much a monument of Arabic prose and debate as a natural history.

Averroes — The Incoherence of the Incoherence

Arabic, c. 1180

A point-by-point reply to al-Ghazali's attack on the philosophers. Averroes quotes Ghazali and answers him, defending the eternity of the world (properly understood), God's knowledge of particulars in a higher mode, and natural causation against Ghazali's occasionalism. Denying real causes, he argues, destroys knowledge itself. He also criticizes Avicenna for deviating from Aristotle and insists that demonstrative philosophy and religion, rightly understood, agree.

Averroes — The Aristotle Commentaries

Arabic, 12th century

Averroes wrote short, middle and long commentaries on nearly all of Aristotle, aiming to recover Aristotle's true meaning from Neoplatonic accretions. The long commentaries (on the Physics, De anima, Metaphysics and others) quote the text section by section with detailed exposition. His views on the unity of the material intellect for all humans caused great controversy in Latin Europe, where he was known simply as "the Commentator."

Avicenna — The Book of Healing (al-Shifa)

Arabic, c. 1020–27

A huge philosophical encyclopedia (healing the soul from ignorance) in four parts: logic, natural sciences (including psychology), mathematics (geometry, arithmetic, music, astronomy), and metaphysics. Highlights include the "floating man" thought experiment showing the soul's self-awareness, the distinction between essence and existence, and the proof of God as the Necessary Existent. It shaped both Islamic and Latin scholastic philosophy.

Ibn Arabi — The Bezels of Wisdom (Fusus al-Hikam)

Arabic, 1229

Twenty-seven chapters, each devoted to a prophet from Adam to Muhammad, each showing a particular divine wisdom set like a gem in a ring. Its core ideas include the unity of being (only God truly exists; creatures are his self-manifestations), the divine names as the source of the world's diversity, the Perfect Human as mirror of God, and the relativity of beliefs (each person worships the God of their belief). It is Ibn Arabi's most studied and most controversial work.

Omar Khayyam — Treatise on Algebra

Arabic, c. 1070

A systematic study of cubic equations. Khayyam classifies them into types and solves each geometrically by intersecting conic sections (parabolas, circles, hyperbolas), finding the positive roots. He notes that an arithmetic (formula) solution eluded him and hoped later mathematicians would find one, which happened in 16th-century Italy. It is a landmark joining algebra and geometry.

21. The Buddhist Canon & Zen

Takuan Sōhō — The Unfettered Mind

Japanese, 17th century. A pre-1930 printed original is at the National Diet Library: dl.ndl.go.jp/pid/859951

Letters from a Zen master to the swordsman Yagyū Munenori. The central teaching is "immovable wisdom": the mind must not stop or fix anywhere, whether on the opponent's sword, one's own technique, or fear. A mind that lingers anywhere is caught. Like water, it should flow freely through the whole body and situation, responding instantly. It applies Zen's no-mind to swordsmanship and daily life.

Yagyū Munenori — The Life-Giving Sword (Heihō Kadensho)

Japanese, 1632

A manual by the sword instructor to the Tokugawa shoguns. The killing sword cuts down evil, but the life-giving sword keeps the peace, and a ruler's strategy is to govern so that fighting is unnecessary. It covers technique (reading the opponent, timing, the "moon in water"), then turns to mind, drawing on Takuan: the practitioner must train until technique is forgotten and acts arise from no-mind.

Dōgen — Shōbōgenzō (Treasury of the True Dharma Eye)

Japanese, 1231–1253

About ninety-five essays by the founder of Sōtō Zen in Japan. Key themes: practice and enlightenment are one, so sitting meditation (shikantaza, "just sitting") is itself enlightenment, not a means to it; being is time (all things are moments of time); every being is Buddha-nature; and the whole world is the true self. It includes practical instructions on washing, cooking and monastic life. Its style is poetic, paradoxical and philosophically deep.

Hakuin — Wild Ivy; the Koan Teachings

Japanese, 18th century

Wild Ivy is Hakuin's spiritual autobiography: childhood terror of hell, years of intense koan practice, breakthrough experiences, a "Zen sickness" (nervous exhaustion) cured by visualization and breathing taught by a hermit, and further awakenings. His teachings revived Rinzai Zen by systematizing koan study, stressing "great doubt" as the engine of awakening, and inventing the koan "What is the sound of one hand?" He urged that insight must continue after awakening.

Eugen Herrigel — Zen in the Art of Archery

German, 1948

A German philosopher's account of six years learning kyūdō (Japanese archery) under master Awa Kenzō in 1920s Japan. Herrigel struggles to release the arrow without deliberate intention. The master insists that "it" should shoot, that the shot must fall like snow from a leaf. After long frustration he experiences effortless release. The book presents art as a path of self-forgetting. It was hugely influential in the West, though scholars now question its accuracy about Zen.

D. T. Suzuki — Zen and Japanese Culture

English, 1938 (revised 1959)

Suzuki argues that Zen shaped Japanese culture: ink painting and its spare, asymmetrical beauty (wabi, sabi), swordsmanship and the samurai spirit, Confucian learning, the tea ceremony, haiku, and love of nature. Throughout he presents Zen as direct intuition beyond concepts. The book defined Western views of Japanese aesthetics, though critics note it idealizes and generalizes.

Shunryu Suzuki — Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind

English, 1970

Edited talks by the Japanese founder of the San Francisco Zen Center. Suzuki teaches that the beginner's mind, open and free of expertise, is the key to practice. Zazen is simply sitting with correct posture and breathing, without seeking attainment. Everyday activities become practice. Themes include nothing special, non-duality, and letting thoughts come and go. It is one of the most widely read introductions to Zen in the West.

The Hundred Thousand Songs of Milarepa

Tibetan, compiled 15th century

A collection of stories and spontaneous songs of the yogi Milarepa (c. 1052–1135) as he meditated in mountain caves and taught hunters, shepherds, demons, scholars and disciples. The songs teach impermanence, renunciation, the uselessness of worldly striving, guru devotion and the nature of mind (Mahamudra), often through vivid images of nature. It is the most beloved book of Tibetan religious literature.

Gampopa — The Jewel Ornament of Liberation

Tibetan, 12th century

A systematic guide to the whole Mahayana path by Milarepa's chief disciple, uniting Kadampa scholarship with Kagyu meditation. It proceeds from the cause (Buddha-nature), the basis (precious human birth), the condition (a spiritual teacher), the method (teachings on impermanence, karma, suffering, love and compassion, bodhicitta, the six perfections, the ten stages), to the result (Buddhahood) and the activities of a Buddha.

Patrul Rinpoche — The Words of My Perfect Teacher

Tibetan, 19th century

A practical, earthy commentary on the preliminary practices of the Longchen Nyingthig tradition. The outer preliminaries are reflections on precious human birth, impermanence, karma and the sufferings of samsara. The inner preliminaries are refuge, bodhicitta, Vajrasattva purification, mandala offering and guru yoga. Patrul uses vivid stories and humor, and constantly exposes the reader's self-deception. It is a standard text for Tibetan Buddhist practitioners.

Tsongkhapa — The Great Treatise on the Stages of the Path (Lamrim Chenmo)

Tibetan, 1402

The foundational text of the Gelug school, based on Atisha's "Lamp for the Path." It organizes all Buddhist teaching by three scopes of motivation: small (seeking a good rebirth), middling (seeking liberation from samsara) and great (seeking Buddhahood for all beings through bodhicitta). The final sections treat calm abiding (shamatha) and special insight (vipashyana) into emptiness, using Madhyamaka reasoning that avoids both nihilism and reification.

22. The Hermetic, the Esoteric & the Mysteries

The Emerald Tablet

Arabic, c. 8th–10th century, Latin from the 12th century

A very short, cryptic text attributed to Hermes Trismegistus, first found in Arabic works such as the Secret of Creation. It proclaims the correspondence of above and below "for the accomplishment of the miracles of the one thing," names the sun as father and moon as mother of that thing, and describes separating the subtle from the gross and a rising to heaven and descending to earth, gaining the glory of the whole world. It became the foundational text of European alchemy (Isaac Newton translated it) and of the hermetic motto "as above, so below."

The Gospel of Thomas

Coptic, found 1945 at Nag Hammadi (Greek fragments earlier)

A collection of 114 sayings attributed to Jesus, without narrative. About half have parallels in the canonical gospels, some in possibly earlier forms, while others are unique and mystical: the kingdom is within and around you, one must know oneself, become a "single one," and find the light within. It emphasizes secret knowledge and interpretation rather than Jesus' death and resurrection. Its date (1st or 2nd century) and relation to Gnosticism are debated.

Zosimos of Panopolis — On the Apparatus and Furnaces

Greek, c. 300 AD

Zosimos is the earliest alchemist whose writings survive. The texts describe distillation apparatus, the kerotakis (a reflux vessel), and processes for coloring and transmuting metals, attributed to earlier authorities such as Maria the Jewess. They also contain visionary dreams in which figures are dismembered and boiled on an altar, allegories of transformation that combine chemistry, Gnostic spirituality and Hermetism.

The Picatrix (Ghayat al-Hakim)

Arabic, 10th–11th century, Latin 1256

A large handbook of astrological magic. It explains a cosmos of spheres whose spirits and rays influence everything below, and teaches how to make talismans at astrologically favorable moments, invoke planetary spirits with prayers, fumigations and colors, and use correspondences between planets, stones, plants and animals. It also includes philosophy on the ranks of knowledge. It deeply influenced Renaissance magic (Ficino, Agrippa).

23. Modern Philosophy & the Depths of the Mind

Søren Kierkegaard — Fear and Trembling

Danish, 1843 (Either/Or is downloaded in Danish)

Writing as "Johannes de Silentio," Kierkegaard meditates on Abraham's willingness to sacrifice Isaac. Ethics is universal, but Abraham's faith involves a "teleological suspension of the ethical": a single individual stands in absolute relation to God above the universal. Faith is not resignation but the "double movement" of giving everything up and then trusting, by virtue of the absurd, to receive it back. Abraham cannot explain himself; faith is paradox, anguish and silence.

Søren Kierkegaard — The Sickness Unto Death

Danish, 1849

Writing as "Anti-Climacus," Kierkegaard analyzes despair as a misrelation within the self, which is a relation that relates itself to itself and to the power that established it. Forms of despair include not knowing one has a self, not willing to be oneself (weakness), and willing to be oneself defiantly. Despair is sin before God. Its cure is faith, in which the self rests transparently in God.

Karl Marx — Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844

German, written 1844, published 1932

Unfinished notebooks where the young Marx develops his theory of alienation. Under capitalism workers are alienated from the product of their labor, from the activity of labor itself, from their "species-being" (free, creative human nature) and from other people. Private property is the result of alienated labor. Communism is imagined as the return of humanity to itself. They also contain critiques of political economy and of Hegel.

Edmund Husserl — Cartesian Meditations

French, 1931 (German 1950)

An introduction to transcendental phenomenology, modeled on Descartes. Through the epoché, the "bracketing" of belief in the world's existence, the philosopher turns to consciousness and its intentional acts (every consciousness is consciousness of something). The meditations describe the constitution of objects, the transcendental ego, and, in the famous fifth meditation, how other minds are experienced through "pairing" and empathy, which grounds an intersubjective world.

Martin Heidegger — Being and Time

German, 1927. The German original should be public domain in the US but wasn't found as a free scan.

Heidegger revives the question of the meaning of being by analyzing Dasein, the human being as the entity for whom being is an issue. Dasein is being-in-the-world, practically engaged with tools ("ready-to-hand") before theorizing ("present-at-hand"). It usually lives inauthentically, absorbed in "the they." Anxiety discloses thrownness and freedom, and being-toward-death makes authentic existence possible. Dasein's being is care, and its meaning is temporality. Only part one was completed.

Ludwig Wittgenstein — Philosophical Investigations

German-English, 1953

Wittgenstein rejects his earlier picture theory of meaning. Meaning is use: words function in many "language games" embedded in forms of life, and concepts like "game" share only family resemblances, not a common essence. Following a rule is a practice, not an inner interpretation. The private language argument claims that a language whose words refer to one's private sensations is incoherent. Philosophy's job is therapy, dissolving confusions that arise when language "goes on holiday."

Jean-Paul Sartre — Being and Nothingness

French, 1943

A long work of existentialist phenomenology. Being-in-itself (things) is solid and complete. Being-for-itself (consciousness) is a nothingness, a lack, always projecting beyond itself. Humans are radically free and "condemned to be free," defining themselves through choice. Denying this freedom is bad faith (the famous waiter who plays at being a waiter). The look of the other turns us into objects, making human relations conflicted. Existential psychoanalysis seeks each person's fundamental project.

C. G. Jung — The Archetypes and the Collective Unconscious

German, essays 1934–1955

Essays arguing that beneath the personal unconscious lies a collective unconscious shared by all humanity. It is structured by archetypes, inherited patterns that appear in dreams, myths and religions: the mother, the child, the trickster, the shadow, the anima and animus, the wise old man, rebirth. The process of individuation integrates these contents into a whole self, often symbolized by mandalas.

C. G. Jung — Aion

German, 1951

Jung explores the self and its symbols, especially Christ as a symbol of the self, and the Christian era as the astrological age of Pisces (the fishes). He analyzes the shadow, anima and animus, and argues that the Christian image of God excluded evil (the Antichrist as Christ's shadow), and that modern culture must integrate the dark side. It draws heavily on Gnosticism, alchemy and astrology.

C. G. Jung — Answer to Job

German, 1952

A passionate, provocative reading of the Book of Job as revealing an unconscious, amoral side of God. Confronted by Job's integrity, God must become conscious and human. Jung sees the incarnation of Christ as God's answer to Job, and later developments (Revelation's wrath, the dogma of Mary's Assumption) as further stages in God's development toward wholeness, which includes the feminine and the dark.

C. G. Jung — Memories, Dreams, Reflections

German, 1962, with Aniela Jaffé

Jung's autobiography of the inner life. It covers childhood dreams and a secret carved manikin, his conflict with his pastor father's faith, medical training, his friendship and break with Freud, his "confrontation with the unconscious" (visions later recorded in the Red Book), travels to Africa, India and the Pueblo peoples, a near-death experience, the Bollingen tower, and late reflections on death and life after death.

Hannah Arendt — The Human Condition

English, 1958

Arendt distinguishes three activities of the vita activa. Labor meets bodily necessity and is endless and consumed. Work fabricates a lasting world of objects. Action, speech and deeds among others, reveals who we are, begins new things and is the stuff of politics. Modernity elevated labor and consumption, lost the public realm and the space of free action, and has retreated into private and scientific perspectives ("world alienation").

Hannah Arendt — The Origins of Totalitarianism

English, 1951

In three parts: modern antisemitism; imperialism, with its racism, bureaucracy and the stateless (people without the "right to have rights"); and totalitarianism. Nazism and Stalinism were a new form of rule based on ideology and terror, mass movements of isolated, lonely individuals, a mobile dual state of party and government, and the concentration camp as the laboratory of total domination, which aims to make humans superfluous.

Simone Weil — Gravity and Grace

French, 1947

A collection of Weil's notebook thoughts, arranged by theme. Gravity is the natural downward pull of the soul toward force, self-interest and illusion. Grace is the supernatural that enters only through a void we make by renouncing. Themes include decreation (undoing the self so God can love creation through us), attention as prayer, affliction, beauty, necessity and the absent God.

Simone Weil — Waiting for God

French, 1950

Letters to the priest Joseph-Marie Perrin, including a spiritual autobiography explaining why she stayed outside the Church while loving Christ, plus essays. "Reflections on the Right Use of School Studies" argues that schoolwork trains attention, the substance of prayer. "The Love of God and Affliction" describes affliction as extreme suffering that uproots life. Others treat implicit forms of love of God (neighbor, world, religious practices, friendship).

24. The Moderns

Franz Kafka — The Castle

German, 1926 (unfinished)

A land surveyor known only as K. arrives in a snowbound village, claiming he was summoned to work for the authorities in the castle above it. He can never reach the castle or get a clear answer from its endless bureaucracy. Villagers defer to officials they never see. K. seduces Frieda, mistress of the official Klamm, hoping for access, and struggles with absurd assistants, a messenger named Barnabas, and the tragic story of Barnabas's family, shunned after his sister refused an official. The novel breaks off unresolved, an image of the search for recognition, grace or meaning from an unreachable authority.

Franz Kafka — Diaries

German, 1910–1923

Kafka's notebooks mix daily notes, dreams, story drafts and fragments, and self-analysis. Recurring themes: the conflict between his office job and the need to write, which he felt as his whole existence; insomnia and illness; his domineering father; failed engagements to Felice Bauer; fascination with Yiddish theater and Jewish identity; and bleak humor. They record the night he wrote "The Judgment" in one sitting.

Yukio Mishima — The Sea of Fertility

Japanese, 1965–1970, four novels

A tetralogy following the lawyer and later judge Honda through twentieth-century Japan as he encounters what he believes are successive reincarnations of his school friend Kiyoaki. In Spring Snow, Kiyoaki is an aristocrat doomed by forbidden love in 1912. In Runaway Horses, Isao is a young nationalist who plans an uprising in the 1930s. In The Temple of Dawn, Ying Chan is a Thai princess. In The Decay of the Angel, Tōru is a cold orphan Honda adopts. Themes: beauty, youth, death, Buddhist ideas of illusion, and Japan's loss of its traditional spirit. It ends in a void that calls all memory into question. Mishima delivered the last manuscript on the day of his ritual suicide.

Yukio Mishima — Confessions of a Mask

Japanese, 1949

A semi-autobiographical novel. The narrator, Kochan, grows up sickly in wartime Tokyo, becomes aware of his attraction to men and his fascination with violent death and martyrdom (Saint Sebastian), and tries to perform "normal" masculinity and feel love for a young woman, Sonoko. He lives behind a mask of conventional behavior, with deep alienation and self-scrutiny.

Yukio Mishima — Sun and Steel

Japanese, 1968

An autobiographical essay on how Mishima, a bookish and frail youth, turned to bodybuilding and martial arts in his thirties. Words, he felt, corrode reality, while muscle and action give direct knowledge of existence. He links physical perfection with the aesthetic of a beautiful death, ending with a flight in a fighter jet, and anticipates the union of pen and sword he sought in his final act.

Yukio Mishima — Patriotism

Japanese, 1961, short story

During the failed 1936 army coup in Tokyo, a young lieutenant learns that close friends are among the rebels and that he will be ordered to attack them. Unable to betray either duty, he chooses ritual suicide with his wife, who joins him. The story describes their last night of love and the suicide in great, disturbing detail, as a union of eros, loyalty and death. Mishima later filmed it himself.

J. R. R. Tolkien — The Hobbit

English, 1937

Bilbo Baggins, a comfort-loving hobbit, is recruited by the wizard Gandalf and thirteen dwarves led by Thorin Oakenshield to help reclaim their treasure and mountain home from the dragon Smaug. On the way he outwits trolls, escapes goblins, wins a riddle contest with Gollum and gains a magic ring, faces giant spiders and the wood-elves, and finds courage. After Smaug's death, greed over the treasure nearly causes war until the Battle of Five Armies. Bilbo returns home changed.

J. R. R. Tolkien — The Lord of the Rings

English, 1954–55

The ring Bilbo found is the One Ring of the dark lord Sauron, which would give him dominion over Middle-earth. Frodo Baggins and a Fellowship of nine set out to destroy it in Mount Doom, where it was forged. The fellowship breaks. Frodo and Sam journey into Mordor, guided by the corrupted Gollum, while Aragorn, Gandalf, Legolas, Gimli, Merry and Pippin fight in Rohan and Gondor. At the last moment Frodo cannot give up the Ring, but Gollum seizes it and falls into the fire. Sauron is defeated, Aragorn is crowned, and the hobbits return to cleanse the Shire. Frodo, wounded beyond healing, departs over the sea. Themes: power and its temptation, mercy, courage of the small, loss and the end of an age.

J. R. R. Tolkien — The Silmarillion

English, 1977 (posthumous)

The mythology behind Middle-earth. The One, Eru Ilúvatar, creates the world through music sung by the Ainur. The rebel Melkor (Morgoth) introduces discord. The Elves awaken, and the elf Fëanor makes three jewels, the Silmarils, holding the light of the Two Trees. Morgoth steals them, and the Noldor elves pursue him to Middle-earth under an oath that brings kinslaying and ruin. The tales include Beren and Lúthien, Túrin Túrambar, and the fall of Gondolin. The book ends with Morgoth's defeat, the rise and drowning of Númenor, and the rings of power.

Louis-Ferdinand Céline — Journey to the End of the Night

French, 1932

Ferdinand Bardamu enlists on a whim in World War I and discovers its idiotic slaughter, then drifts through colonial Africa, the factories of Detroit, and a poor Paris suburb where he works as a doctor. He is shadowed by his double, Robinson. Written in a violent, slangy, spoken style, it presents human life as cowardice, cruelty and misery, with flashes of tenderness. It revolutionized French prose; Céline's later antisemitism stains his legacy.

Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn — One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich

Russian, 1962

One day, from reveille to lights-out, in a Soviet labor camp, seen through Ivan Denisovich Shukhov, a peasant sentenced on false charges. He counts extra bread, soup bowls and small victories, lays bricks with his work team in bitter cold, and preserves dignity through work, cunning and solidarity. The understated tone makes the injustice more powerful. Its publication under Khrushchev was a sensation.

Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn — The Gulag Archipelago

Russian, 1973

A massive "experiment in literary investigation" of the Soviet prison camp system, drawn from Solzhenitsyn's own imprisonment and testimony from over two hundred survivors. It describes arrest, interrogation and torture, transport, and the history of the camps from Lenin's time. It shows forced labor and death, the thieves and informers, and prisoners' resistance and inner freedom. A central insight is that the line between good and evil runs through every human heart. It helped destroy Soviet communism's moral authority.

Ernst Jünger — On the Marble Cliffs

German, 1939

The narrator and his brother, veterans, live as botanists in a hermitage above the peaceful Marina region. From the forests beyond, the Head Forester and his thugs gradually undermine the ordered civilization through terror, corruption and nihilistic violence, culminating in a hideous torture site and the burning of the Marina. The brothers witness, resist and flee. It is widely read as an allegory of Nazism, published under the regime itself.

Albert Camus — The Stranger

French, 1942

Meursault, a French clerk in Algiers, records events flatly: his mother's funeral, where he does not cry; an affair with Marie; helping his neighbor Raymond; and, on a blazing beach, shooting an Arab man for no clear reason. At his trial he is condemned less for the killing than for his indifference and failure to show conventional feeling. Awaiting execution, he rejects the chaplain and accepts the "benign indifference" of the universe, feeling at peace. It embodies the absurd.

Albert Camus — The Myth of Sisyphus

French, 1942

An essay beginning from the question of whether life is worth living. The absurd is the clash between humanity's need for meaning and the universe's silence. Camus rejects suicide and also "philosophical suicide" (leaping into faith or systems). Instead one should live with the absurd through revolt, freedom and passion. Sisyphus, eternally pushing his rock, is the absurd hero: aware of his fate yet scornful of it, one must imagine him happy.

Hermann Hesse — The Glass Bead Game

German, 1943

In a future province, Castalia, an intellectual order devotes itself to the Glass Bead Game, an abstract synthesis of all arts and sciences. Joseph Knecht rises through the order to become Magister Ludi (master of the game). He comes to feel that Castalia is sterile, detached from history and life, resigns to become a tutor in the world, and dies soon after. The book includes his student poems and three "lives" he wrote. Themes: contemplation versus action, spirit and service.

Georges Bernanos — The Diary of a Country Priest

French, 1936

The diary of a young, sickly priest in a poor, indifferent village in northern France. He struggles with his own inadequacy, the parishioners' spiritual boredom, and hostility from the gentry, and in a great scene he brings a despairing countess to peace before her death. He discovers he has stomach cancer and dies in a friend's apartment, saying that grace is everywhere. A portrait of holiness in weakness.

Evelyn Waugh — Brideshead Revisited

English, 1945

Captain Charles Ryder, billeted during World War II at the great house of Brideshead, remembers his involvement with the aristocratic Catholic Flyte family: his Oxford friendship with the charming Sebastian, who descends into alcoholism; his later love affair with Sebastian's sister Julia; and the family's struggles with their faith. At the end the dying Lord Marchmain accepts the last rites, and Charles discovers faith himself. The theme is divine grace working through human lives.

C. S. Lewis — The Abolition of Man

English, 1943

Three lectures beginning with a critique of an English textbook that teaches that value judgments are only about the speaker's feelings. Lewis argues that there is an objective moral order (the Tao, recognized across civilizations) and that education should train emotions to respond to it rightly, producing "men with chests." If values are debunked as subjective, those who control technology and conditioning will reshape humanity according to their whims. Humanity's conquest of nature becomes nature's conquest of humanity.

C. S. Lewis — Till We Have Faces

English, 1956

A retelling of the myth of Cupid and Psyche narrated by Orual, the ugly elder sister of the beautiful Psyche. When Psyche is offered to the god of the mountain and claims to live in an invisible palace as his wife, Orual, in possessive love and doubt, forces her to break the god's command, and Psyche is exiled. Orual becomes a veiled queen and writes her complaint against the gods, only to discover in visions her own devouring love. Themes: love, jealousy, faith, and seeing oneself truly.

T. S. Eliot — Four Quartets

English, 1936–1942

Four long meditative poems, each named for a place: Burnt Norton (a rose garden), East Coker (Eliot's ancestral village), The Dry Salvages (rocks off Massachusetts), and Little Gidding (a religious community). Each follows a five-part musical structure and is loosely associated with one of the four elements. They meditate on time and eternity, memory, the limits of language, spiritual humility and the intersection of the timeless with time. They end in a vision of reconciliation, drawing on Christian mysticism.

Rainer Maria Rilke — Duino Elegies

German, 1923. The German original is public domain but wasn't found as a free scan.

Ten elegies begun in 1912 at Duino Castle and completed in 1922. They lament human limitation: we are transient, divided and never at home in the world, unlike animals and the terrifying angels, who are pure being. Figures include lovers, heroes, children, the dead who die young, and acrobats. The later elegies turn toward affirmation: our task is to "say" things, transform the visible world into the invisible within us, and praise. Many consider them the high point of modern German poetry.

Thomas Mann — Doctor Faustus

German, 1947

Narrated by the humanist Serenus Zeitblom, the life of the composer Adrian Leverkühn, a brilliant, cold modernist who contracts syphilis on purpose and, in a feverish dialogue, makes a pact with the devil: twenty-four years of creative genius in exchange for renouncing love. He produces revolutionary music based on the twelve-tone method (a source of controversy with Schoenberg) and ends in madness. His fate is set against Germany's plunge into Nazism.

William Faulkner — Absalom, Absalom!

English, 1936

The story of Thomas Sutpen, a poor man who arrives in Mississippi in 1833 with enslaved workers and builds a plantation and dynasty. It collapses through his repudiation of his first wife (who had African ancestry) and their son Charles Bon, Bon's friendship with Sutpen's son Henry and engagement to Sutpen's daughter, fratricide, and the Civil War. It is told decades later, piecemeal and contradictorily, by several narrators trying to reconstruct it. It is a tragedy of the South, race, and the burden of history.

Fernando Pessoa — The Book of Disquiet

Portuguese, fragments written c. 1913–1935, published 1982

A collection of hundreds of fragmentary reflections attributed to Bernardo Soares, an assistant bookkeeper in Lisbon who lives in a rented room and dreams. He writes about tedium, the rain and streets of Lisbon, the unreality of the self, the superiority of dreaming to acting, and the pain and beauty of consciousness. There is no plot. It reads as a "factless autobiography" and a study of the mind's inner weather.

Fernando Pessoa — The Heteronym Poems

Portuguese, 1914–1935

Pessoa wrote as several fully imagined poets with their own biographies and styles. Alberto Caeiro is a pastoral master of pure sensation who rejects metaphysics. Ricardo Reis is a classical Stoic-Epicurean writing Horatian odes. Álvaro de Campos is a modernist engineer whose poems range from ecstatic futurism (the "Maritime Ode") to despair ("Tobacco Shop"). Pessoa also wrote under his own name ("Message," a mystical nationalist sequence). Together they explore the multiplicity of the self.

Pablo Neruda — Canto General

Spanish, 1950

A vast epic of the Americas in fifteen sections. It begins with the land, rivers, plants and animals before humans, then honors the pre-Columbian peoples ("The Heights of Macchu Picchu"), the conquistadors and liberators, and the working people, while denouncing dictators, oligarchs and foreign corporations. It includes Neruda's own flight from Chile and his communist political vision, and ends with his personal testament. A work of political poetry and myth.

Giuseppe Tomasi di Lampedusa — The Leopard

Italian, 1958 (posthumous)

Sicily, 1860, as Garibaldi's forces unify Italy. Don Fabrizio, Prince of Salina, a proud, ironic, astronomy-loving aristocrat, watches his class decline. His nephew Tancredi joins the revolution, reasoning that everything must change so that everything can stay the same, and marries Angelica, beautiful daughter of a rich, vulgar upstart. Don Fabrizio accepts the new order with melancholy, declines a senate seat, and dies decades later. It is an elegy for a world, full of sensuous detail.

Yasunari Kawabata — Snow Country

Japanese, 1935–1947

Shimamura, a wealthy, idle Tokyo dilettante, visits a hot-spring town in the snowy mountains of western Japan, where he has an affair with the geisha Komako. She loves him intensely and wholeheartedly, while he remains detached, observing her as he observes nature. He is also fascinated by another young woman, Yoko. The novel is spare and imagistic, ending with a fire in a cocoon warehouse. Themes: wasted love, beauty and loneliness.

25. The Forbidden Shelf

Julius Evola — Revolt Against the Modern World

Italian, 1934

Evola contrasts two worlds. The "world of Tradition" was a hierarchical, sacred order ruled by priest-kings, with castes, initiation, a solar masculine spirituality, and contact with a transcendent reality. The modern world is its degeneration through the four ages (gold to iron, the Kali Yuga): the loss of sacred kingship, the rise of merchants and masses, democracy, materialism and individualism. He urges readers to reject modernity inwardly. It draws on René Guénon but adds racial and aristocratic politics; it influenced Italian Fascism's fringes and later far-right movements.

Julius Evola — Ride the Tiger

Italian, 1961

A manual for the "differentiated man" living in the Kali Yuga, when traditional institutions have collapsed and political action seems useless. Rather than fight modernity head on, such a person should "ride the tiger": use the chaotic forces of the age (nihilism, the collapse of values, modern art and science) to strengthen inner detachment and self-mastery. He discusses Nietzsche, existentialism and modern music. It is the most individualist of his works.

Julius Evola — Men Among the Ruins

Italian, 1953

A post-war political treatise that criticizes democracy, capitalism, communism and nationalism as symptoms of decline. Evola proposes a traditionalist "organic" state based on spiritual authority, hierarchy and an elite order. He argues that the right should become a true "Right" of principles rather than a party. It was influential on Italian neo-fascist circles.

Carl Schmitt — The Concept of the Political

German, 1927 article, 1932 book

Schmitt argues that the political is defined by the distinction between friend and enemy, the possibility of collective conflict, just as morality has good and evil and aesthetics has beautiful and ugly. The state is the entity that decides who the enemy is and can demand that its members risk death. Liberalism tries to dissolve the political into economics and ethics, but cannot abolish it. It is a key text of political realism, controversial because of Schmitt's later Nazi membership.

Carl Schmitt — Political Theology

German, 1922. The original is public domain but wasn't found as a free scan.

It opens by defining the sovereign as the one who decides on the exception, the emergency beyond normal rules. Law cannot fully govern the exception, so sovereignty is revealed in the decision. Schmitt also argues that the significant concepts of modern theory of the state are secularized theological concepts (the omnipotent lawgiver as a secularized God, the exception as a miracle). He criticizes liberal parliamentarism and admires counter-revolutionary thinkers (de Maistre, Donoso Cortés).

E. M. Cioran — A Short History of Decay

French, 1949

Cioran's first French book, a collection of aphorisms and short essays. He attacks all faith and fanaticism, including religions, ideologies and even the will to believe, as the root of cruelty. He praises doubt, lucidity and idleness, and explores boredom, melancholy, the futility of history and the vanity of action. The style is elegant, bitter and ironic. Existence is a fall from the peace of non-being.

E. M. Cioran — The Trouble with Being Born

French, 1973

Aphorisms centered on the idea that the true catastrophe is not death but birth, coming into existence at all. Cioran reflects on insomnia, consciousness as a wound, the illusion of the self, Buddhism, Gnosticism, failed saints and the temptation of suicide (which he considers but never commits). Despite its darkness it is often witty and consoling in its extremity.
